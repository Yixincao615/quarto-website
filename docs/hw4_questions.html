MaxDiff Analysis of MSBA Class Preferences

Counts, MLE, and Bayesian estimation of the MNL model

Author

Yixin Cao

Published

May 28, 2026

Introduction

MaxDiff, also called Best-Worst Scaling, is a survey method used to measure relative preference. In each task, a respondent sees a small subset of items, in this case four MSBA classes, and chooses the item they prefer most and the item they prefer least. Because respondents repeatedly make best-worst choices, MaxDiff creates direct trade-offs between the items.

MaxDiff is useful because people are usually better at making comparisons than assigning numeric ratings. A 1–10 rating scale can be hard to interpret because different people use rating scales differently. MaxDiff forces respondents to choose winners and losers within each set, which reduces scale-use bias and creates clearer preference differences.

In this assignment, the ten items are the ten core MSBA classes at UCSD Rady. I estimate class preferences in three ways. First, I use a simple counts analysis based on the percentage of times each class was picked best minus the percentage of times it was picked worst. Second, I estimate a multinomial logit model using maximum likelihood. Third, I estimate the same MNL model using Bayesian methods with a Metropolis-Hastings sampler.

I expect the three methods to agree on the general ranking of classes, especially for the most-preferred and least-preferred items. They may differ more in the middle of the ranking, where preferences are closer and small differences can change the order.

\[ \text{score}_j = \%\text{best}_j - \%\text{worst}_j \]

A high positive score means the class was frequently selected as best. A negative score means the class was more often selected as worst.

From MaxDiff Data to MNL Choices

The MaxDiff survey can be converted into multinomial logit (MNL) choice observations. Each survey task provides two separate choices.

First, the respondent selects the most preferred item from the four classes shown on the screen. This creates a standard multinomial logit choice among four alternatives.

Second, after removing the selected best item, the respondent chooses the least preferred item from the remaining three alternatives. Following the approach discussed in class, the worst choice is modeled as a best choice among the remaining alternatives with utilities multiplied by -1.

The MNL model assigns each class a utility coefficient \(\beta_j\). For a task showing items \(\{a,b,c,d\}\), the probability that item \(b\) is selected as best is:

\[ P(\text{best}=b) = \frac{\exp(\beta_b)} {\exp(\beta_a)+\exp(\beta_b)+\exp(\beta_c)+\exp(\beta_d)} \]

After removing the best item, the probability that item \(c\) is selected as worst is:

\[ P(\text{worst}=c \mid \text{best}=b) = \frac{\exp(-\beta_c)} {\exp(-\beta_a)+\exp(-\beta_c)+\exp(-\beta_d)} \]

Because adding the same constant to every utility does not change the probabilities, the model is not fully identified. Therefore, one coefficient must be fixed as a reference level. I set:

\[ \beta_1 = 0 \]

and estimate the remaining nine coefficients.

Before fitting the MNL model, I first verify that every task contains exactly:

  • Four items shown
  • One best choice
  • One worst choice

Any incomplete or invalid tasks are removed before estimation.

MNL via Maximum Likelihood

The multinomial logit (MNL) model estimates a utility coefficient for each class. Classes with larger utility coefficients are more likely to be selected as the best choice. The model uses both the best and worst selections from each MaxDiff task.

The log-likelihood for the MaxDiff MNL model is:

\[ \ell(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \sum_{\text{tasks}} \left[ \beta_{j_b^*} - \log \sum_{j \in \text{shown}} \exp(\beta_j) - \beta_{j_w^*} - \log \sum_{j \in \text{shown}\setminus\{j_b^*\}} \exp(-\beta_j) \right] \]

where:

  • \(j_b^*\) is the item selected as best
  • \(j_w^*\) is the item selected as worst

The first term models the best choice from the full set of four classes. The second term models the worst choice after removing the selected best item.

To identify the model, the utility of Class 1 is fixed at zero and the remaining nine utility coefficients are estimated.

J_total = 10

def make_beta_full(beta_free):
    return np.concatenate([[0], beta_free])

def log_likelihood(beta_free, task_data):

    beta = make_beta_full(beta_free)

    total_ll = 0.0

    for _, row in task_data.iterrows():

        shown = np.array(row["shown"], dtype=int) - 1

        best = int(row["best"]) - 1
        worst = int(row["worst"]) - 1

        beta_shown = beta[shown]

        # Best choice probability
        ll_best = beta[best] - logsumexp(beta_shown)

        # Worst choice probability
        remaining = shown[shown != best]

        ll_worst = (
            -beta[worst]
            - logsumexp(-beta[remaining])
        )

        total_ll += ll_best + ll_worst

    return total_ll


def neg_log_likelihood(beta_free, task_data):
    return -log_likelihood(beta_free, task_data)

Estimate the model using the BFGS optimization algorithm.

out = minimize(

    x0=np.zeros(J_total - 1),

Convert utilities into shares of preference.

The share of preference for class \(j\) is:

\[ \hat s_j = \frac{\exp(\hat\beta_j)} {\sum_{k=1}^{10} \exp(\hat\beta_k)} \]

These shares sum to one and can be interpreted as the predicted share of choices each class would receive if all ten classes were shown together.

The MNL model produced results that were highly consistent with the counts analysis. Marketing Analytics received the largest estimated share of preference (28.4%), substantially higher than all other classes. Business Analytics and Customer Analytics ranked second and third, respectively, while Analytics Consulting received the lowest share of preference.

The ranking from the MNL model was identical to the ranking from the counts analysis. This suggests that students have relatively clear preferences among the core MSBA classes and that the simpler counts approach already captures much of the underlying preference structure.

The MNL model nevertheless provides additional value because it explicitly models the choice process and converts utility estimates into shares of preference. These shares can be interpreted as the predicted proportion of choices each class would receive if all ten classes were presented simultaneously.

MNL via Bayesian Estimation

The Bayesian MNL model uses the same likelihood as the maximum likelihood model but combines it with a prior distribution on the utility coefficients.

Using Bayes’ rule, the posterior distribution is:

\[ \pi(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid \boldsymbol{y}) \propto L(\boldsymbol{\beta}) \cdot \pi(\boldsymbol{\beta}) \]

where:

  • (L()) is the MNL likelihood
  • (()) is the prior distribution

I use a weakly informative normal prior:

\[ \beta_j \sim N(0,10) \]

This prior is intentionally broad so that the observed data dominate the estimation.

Posterior Function

def log_prior(beta_free):

    return np.sum(
        -0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi * 10)
        - (beta_free ** 2) / (2 * 10)
    )

def log_posterior(beta_free, task_data):

    return (
        log_likelihood(beta_free, task_data)
        +
        log_prior(beta_free)
    )

Metropolis-Hastings Sampler

I use a random-walk Metropolis-Hastings sampler. At each iteration, the algorithm proposes a new parameter vector and decides whether to accept or reject it based on the posterior density.

def mh_sampler(beta0, n_iter, step, task_data):

    K = len(beta0)

    draws = np.zeros((n_iter, K))

    beta = beta0.copy()

    lp = log_posterior(beta, task_data)

    accept = 0

    for t in range(n_iter):

        beta_star = (
            beta
            +
            np.random.normal(
                0,
                step,
                size=K
            )
        )

        lp_star = log_posterior(
            beta_star,
            task_data
        )

        if np.log(np.random.rand()) < (lp_star - lp):

            beta = beta_star
            lp = lp_star
            accept += 1

        draws[t] = beta

    return {
        "draws": draws,
        "accept_rate": accept / n_iter
    }

The counts analysis provides a straightforward descriptive summary of preferences based on observed best and worst selections. The MNL model extends this approach by explicitly modeling the choice process and incorporating information from every item shown in each task, not only the items selected. As a result, the MNL model produces utility estimates and shares of preference that are grounded in a formal behavioral framework.

The Bayesian MNL model produces estimates that are nearly identical to the MLE results, indicating that the observed data contain a strong preference signal and that the weakly informative prior has minimal influence on the final rankings. The primary advantage of the Bayesian approach is the ability to quantify uncertainty through posterior credible intervals, providing additional insight into the reliability of the estimated preferences.

Overall, the consistency across the counts analysis, maximum likelihood estimation, and Bayesian estimation suggests that the preference ranking is robust and not driven by any particular estimation method. This increases confidence that the identified ranking reflects genuine student preferences rather than methodological artifacts.

Discussion

This analysis shows that MSBA students have clear differences in their preferences across the ten core classes. Marketing Analytics received the strongest preference signal, while Analytics Consulting received the weakest preference signal in this survey.

The three methods serve different purposes. Counts analysis is best for a quick descriptive summary. MLE MNL is better for formal model-based estimation. Bayesian MNL is useful when uncertainty is important, especially for quantities like preference shares.

There are several limitations. The survey reflects the preferences of this sample of MSBA students, so the results may not generalize to all students. Preferences may also depend on prior coursework, career goals, instructors, or the quarter in which students took the survey.

If I had more time, I would extend the model to estimate individual-level preferences or compare preferences across student segments. I would also test whether students with more technical backgrounds prefer different classes than students with more business-oriented backgrounds.
